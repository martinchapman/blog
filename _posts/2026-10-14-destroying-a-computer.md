This scene and the concepts behind it are appealing for several reasons.

In the first instance, as with the rest of the series, the writers focus on producing an [accurate representation](https://tomaytotomato.com/mr-robot-and-destroying-your-pc/) of what such a process would look like in real life[^1].

More broadly though, and the fact that in this television series the destruction is often linked to illegal activity aside, there is something that feels very healthy about the concept of a hard digital reset; assuming it is followed by the setting up of a new device, things that weren't important enough to remember aren't restored, for example, which feels much cleaner than the common approach of having a monolithic digital setup that is copied verbatim between devices (likely facilitated by a paid third-party).
While I won't be willingly destroying my own hardware any time soon, one can follow this practice by wiping a phone and starting fresh on a new one, or even by simply closing all the current windows open on a machine at any given time and re-approaching a task with less visual noise.

A related, appealing concept is the idea that following the destruction it is possible to (cleanly) restore the digital setup held previously.
This reduces the dependency on any given device.
Although the character of Elliot would likely restore his environment purely from memory, for me support is needed to achieve this clean-but-restorable setup, in the form of [infrastructure as code (IaC)](https://www.atlassian.com/microservices/cloud-computing/infrastructure-as-code).
Notably the lack of dependency on a device, and the willingness to reset it, does not extend to the data held on that device, where I very much remain a [data hoarder](https://www.reddit.com/r/DataHoarder/).

[^1]: Other scenes, including the use of a [Raspberry Pi in a hack](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=XTN_-pRZjoU), are equally realistic.
